TCAE 2026 · TORONTO

Executive conference summary

Overview

The 2026 Toronto Compliance & AML Conference followed financial intelligence from the front line to the boardroom. Across banking, digital assets and gaming, the recurring question was whether an institution can recognize a change in risk, get the evidence to someone with authority and act while the outcome can still change.

The first four sections cover Canada’s AML regime, board oversight and FINTRAC supervision. The remaining sections look at stablecoin settlement, AI-assisted decisions, casino transactions and fraud disruption. Each section pairs the discussion with a practical question drawn from the fieldnotes.

Section 1

How does Canada know its AML regime is working?

After the opening remarks, Sarah Paquet’s keynote and fireside conversation brought the public-sector perspective into the room. The subsequent discussion with Jeremy Weil turned to the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) mutual evaluation. These sessions put two levels of effectiveness in view. A reporting entity can show that it filed a report. A financial intelligence unit needs facts it can connect to other information, and investigators need leads they can corroborate.

Practical takeaway Follow one lead. Can a reader reconstruct who moved value, when, through which parties, and why the activity was suspicious? A useful account also identifies what remains unknown.

Section 2

What changes for the business when the rules change?

Ora Morison’s Canadian regulatory update raised a practical issue for compliance teams. A proposal, an enacted amendment and a provision already in force can appear in the same board briefing. Each calls for a different decision about timing, ownership and implementation.

Practical takeaway For each development, record its status, effective date, affected activity and control owner. Give management a clear account of what needs action now and what still needs verification.

Section 3

Can directors see the risk behind the backlog?

The financial-institutions panel examined the difficulty of running a risk-based programme across business lines. A rising alert queue can mean heavier demand, poorly targeted rules or a break in the underlying data. Each explanation points to a different investment. Directors need the affected population, the consequences of delay and credible options for reducing exposure.

Practical takeaway A board paper should show the compliance recommendation, the decision taken, the person accountable and the date on which directors will see evidence of improvement.

Section 4

Can a reviewer reproduce the control decision?

The FINTRAC supervision and enforcement discussion focused on what institutions can demonstrate during an examination. A policy describes intended practice. A transaction sample shows which rule ran, what staff knew and how they handled an exception. During the examination, dated records give the institution a basis to correct factual misunderstandings and explain remediation already underway.

Practical takeaway Trace one ordinary case and one exception from source record to disposition. If either reveals a gap, establish its scope and independently retest the repair before closing it.

Section 5

At what point has the customer actually been paid?

The digital-assets panel examined stablecoins and tokenization across regulatory and operational boundaries. A token transfer may be final on-chain while the recipient is still waiting for spendable local currency. Tokenization also raises a question about rights: the ledger entry, the legal claim and the party able to honour it may sit in different places.

Practical takeaway Start with the holder’s claim. Map the issuer, custodian and payout partner, then reconcile the on-chain transfer to the off-chain record that establishes delivery or redemption.

Section 6

Which decisions can a tool support?

The AI governance discussion connected model oversight with everyday compliance work. A screening service can appear healthy after a source feed stops updating. A fluent case summary can turn an uncertain wallet association into a confident allegation. The consequences depend on what the tool is permitted to influence and whether a reviewer can recover its sources.

Practical takeaway Define the permitted use and the fallback. Test source coverage, keep evidence for material claims, and give a named reviewer authority to correct an output or suspend the tool.

Section 7

What does the player’s activity actually show?

The casino discussion illustrated the limits of interpreting a transaction in isolation. In the example of a player using two adjacent machines, movement between machines alone tells an investigator little. Deposits, actual play, incentives, cash-out and staff observations can support different explanations, from ordinary play to fraud, laundering or gambling harm.

Practical takeaway Build one timeline, test competing explanations and route supported concerns to the teams authorized to act. A player may need both a financial crime review and a separate support response.

Section 8

Who can still interrupt the loss?

The law-enforcement panel brought the day back to time. A telecom signal, a bank transfer and a crypto destination can each reveal a different part of a suspected scam. A verified handoff can help a partner intervene while funds are still reachable. The institution also has a distinct AML decision to make. The formal programme concluded with awards and closing remarks before the reception.

Practical takeaway Track the time from signal to authorized intervention. Record funds held, recovered and actually returned separately so a temporary hold is never counted as a customer refund.
